SECTION 2  —  How to use this record
2.1  Who it is for
Complete this record with a patient (or, if the patient lacks capacity, with the surrogate decision-maker) when the answer to the surprise question — “Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next 12 months?” — is “no”, and at least one of the following is present:
–  Advanced disease with no remaining disease-modifying options, or options the patient has declined.
–  Progressive and continuing decline in general condition: poor performance status, largely bed-bound, minimal oral intake, disinterested or withdrawn.
–  Repeated unplanned admissions for the same underlying problem.
–  The patient or family has asked about what will happen at the end, or about avoiding hospital.
The NCG End-of-Life Care algorithm adds, for the actively dying phase: deteriorating vital signs — low blood pressure, cold skin, jaw breathing or Cheyne–Stokes respiration. Where the signs are ambiguous, take a second opinion before acting on them.
2.2  What this record is, and what it is not
IT IS | IT IS NOT
–  A summary of what this patient values and what they would and would not accept, agreed in conversation and signed. / –  A prompt for the emergency clinician to make a recommendation that fits the patient, quickly. / –  Patient-held and portable: it travels with the patient between home, ambulance, ED, ward and ICU. / –  Revisable at any time, by the patient or by the clinical team as the condition changes. | –  Not, by itself, a legally executed Advance Medical Directive under the Supreme Court framework (see Appendix C). It can be converted into one, or can sit alongside one. / –  Not authority to withdraw life-sustaining treatment that has already been started. Withdrawal in India follows the Primary and Secondary Medical Board process (Appendix C). / –  Not a mechanism to demand treatment the clinical team judges non-beneficial. A patient or family may refuse a treatment; they cannot require one that is not indicated. / –  Not a DNACPR form. A decision not to attempt CPR says nothing about any other treatment.


2.3  Sequence for completing it
# | Step | Notes
1 | Confirm eligibility and capacity | Sections 3 and 4. Capacity is decision-specific and can fluctuate; assess it now, not from the notes.
2 | Establish what is understood | Section 5. Do not start on treatment choices until the family can say back, in their own words, that the illness is not curable.
3 | Elicit values before options | Section 6. What matters, and what outcomes would be unacceptable. Preferences recorded without this step are unreliable.
4 | Make a recommendation | Sections 7–14. Offer a recommendation based on the values just heard — not an unguided menu. Then record what is agreed.
5 | Record what will always continue | Section 15. Say this out loud: care is not being withdrawn, only the treatments that are no longer helping.
6 | Sign, copy, distribute, and set a review date | Sections 17 and 18. A record nobody can find in an emergency has no effect.

SECTION 10  —  The time-limited trial: deciding in advance when to stop
A time-limited trial is an agreement between the family and the team to use life-sustaining treatment for a defined period, after which the patient’s response — measured against markers agreed now, in advance — decides whether treatment continues or the focus shifts entirely to comfort. Reported trials commonly run about 3 days, with a usual range of roughly 3 to 7. Agreeing the markers before the treatment starts is what makes the later conversation possible; without them, every review becomes a fresh argument about whether to “give up”.

Procedural note — read before acting on the box above / In India, withdrawing life-sustaining treatment already in progress is lawful but procedural. Following the Supreme Court’s order of 24 January 2023 and the MoHFW draft guidelines, the hospital constitutes a Primary Medical Board (the treating physician plus at least two subject experts with five or more years’ experience), which explains the illness, the options and the consequences of continuing or stopping to the surrogate; a Secondary Medical Board (a practitioner nominated by the District Chief Medical Officer plus at least two experts, none of whom sat on the Primary Board) then reviews, and is directed to give its opinion within 48 hours. This record captures the family’s wish in advance; it does not replace that process, and the treating team must initiate it. Verify the current position before the pilot goes live — the draft guidelines were still in consultation and the Supreme Court returned to the subject in March 2026.

SECTION 12  —  Feeding and fluids
To be explained before this section is completed / As the body shuts down, the need for food and fluid falls. Feeding a dying patient more does not extend life and often causes harm. The NCG algorithm is explicit: match fluids to urine output, allow oral sips as tolerated, keep parenteral fluid to no more than about 500–1000 mL of normal saline in 24 hours, and avoid over-hydration — which causes aspiration, oedema, bladder distension and breathlessness. Families experience “no feeding” as abandonment or starvation. Name that fear directly, explain the physiology, and promise what will continue: mouth care, sips, taste, and the offer of food by hand whenever the patient wants it.

SECTION 18  —  Changing this record, and resolving disagreement
18.1  Changing or cancelling it
–  A patient with capacity can change or cancel any part of this record at any time, verbally. Their current wish always overrides what is written here.
–  A surrogate can ask for a review at any time.
–  To cancel: strike through the page, write CANCELLED with the date and signature, and destroy the copies held by the family and the ambulance service. Record the cancellation in the hospital notes.

18.2  When the family disagrees among themselves
–  Do not proceed on a contested decision. Convene a single family meeting with the named surrogate and the dissenting members present together, with the treating consultant and palliative care.
–  Return the discussion to what the patient would have wanted, not to what each member wants.
–  If it remains unresolved: refer to the institutional clinical ethics committee. Continue current treatment meanwhile.
–  Watch for the member who arrives late from another city and reverses an agreed plan — bring them into a full conversation rather than reversing course on a phone call.

18.3  When the team judges a requested treatment to be non-beneficial
–  The team is not obliged to provide a treatment it judges will not benefit the patient. Explain why, in plain language, without using the word “futile” as the whole of the explanation.
–  Offer a second opinion from a clinician outside the treating team, and offer transfer if the family wishes it.
–  Document the reasoning, who was present, and what was offered.

APPENDIX A  —  Conducting the conversation
The NCG algorithm names SPIKES and Ask–Tell–Ask, and directs that information be mixed with the maintenance of hope, that uncertainty be emphasised, and that survival be discussed in probabilistic terms. It also warns, in the patient panel, against philosophising and against comparing this patient with others.
STEP | WHAT TO DO | WORDS THAT WORK
Set up | A private space, everyone seated, phones silenced, the key family members present at the same time. Say how long you have. | “I’ve asked you all to come together so we can talk properly. I have about twenty minutes and no interruptions.”
Ask | Find out what they already understand before you say anything. This is the single most skipped step. | “What have the doctors told you so far?”  “What is your own sense of how things are going?”
Permission | Ask before delivering the substance. | “Would it be all right if I told you what I’m worried about?”
Tell | A short warning shot, then the headline in one plain sentence. No jargon, no numbers yet. Then stop and be quiet. | “I’m afraid I have difficult news.”  … “The cancer is no longer responding, and I think he is dying.”
Respond to emotion | Silence, then name the emotion. Do not fill the gap with facts. Nothing said in the next two minutes will be remembered as information. | “I can see this is very hard.”  “Tell me what is going through your mind.”
Ask again | Check what landed before adding more. | “What is your understanding of what I’ve just said?”
Map values | Move from treatments to outcomes. This is what fills Section 6. | “What would he say is most important to him now?”  “Is there anything he would not want to go through?”
Recommend | Do not hand them a menu. Make a recommendation grounded in what they just told you, and give them room to disagree. | “Given what you’ve told me matters to him, I would recommend we … What do you think of that?”
Close the loop | Summarise, write it down in front of them, say what happens next and who to call. | “Let me read back what I’ve written, and you tell me if I’ve got it wrong.”


A.1  Phrases to avoid, and what to say instead
AVOID | INSTEAD
“Do you want us to do everything?” — the only possible answer is yes, and it asks the family to authorise a death. | “What we do next should follow from what matters most to him. Here is what I recommend, and why.”
“There is nothing more we can do.” — untrue, and heard as abandonment. | “We cannot stop the illness now. There is a great deal we can still do for how he feels and how this time goes.”
“We will withdraw care.” | “We will stop the treatments that are no longer helping him, and step up everything that keeps him comfortable.”
“Shall we stop feeding him?” | “His body can no longer use food the way it did. Forcing it in now causes more trouble than it prevents. We will keep giving him tastes and sips and keep his mouth comfortable.”
“He has two months.” | “Patients in his situation usually have weeks to a few months. Some do better, some much less. I would use this time as though it were short.”
“It is your decision.” — leaves the family carrying the death. | “This is our decision together. Based on what you’ve told me, my recommendation is …”
Comparing with another patient, or philosophising about death. | Stay with this patient and this family. Listen more than you explain.


A.2  Two questions that unlock most of this form
–  “If time is short, what would you want it spent on?”
–  “Is there anything that would be worse for him than dying?” — this is the question that produces Section 6.2, and it is the one that makes ceilings of care make sense to a family.

APPENDIX B  —  Recognising the dying phase
From the NCG End-of-Life Care algorithm. Recognition triggers the completion, or the urgent review, of this record.
BACKGROUND STATE | SIGNS IN THE LAST DAYS
–  Advanced stage disease with no disease-modifying options remaining / –  Constantly worsening general condition / –  Poor functional status; bed-ridden / –  No feeds; disinterested or withdrawn | –  Falling blood pressure / –  Cold skin, mottled peripheries / –  Jaw breathing / –  Cheyne–Stokes breathing / –  Reduced urine output


Where the signs are ambiguous, take a second opinion before acting on them. Recognition of dying is a clinical judgement that is wrong often enough to warrant a check — and a wrongly declared dying phase produces a self-fulfilling outcome.


B.1  Once the dying phase is recognised
–  Complete the communication tasks: ensure insight, document what was discussed and with whom.
–  De-escalate invasive interventions that no longer serve comfort.
–  Simultaneously plan routine care and review it daily: position, skin and pressure-sore prevention, bowel and bladder, mouth care, fluids matched to output.
–  Reassess this record in full — sections written weeks earlier may no longer fit.
APPENDIX C  —  The Indian legal and procedural position
This appendix is a working summary for clinicians, current to the sources cited and not a substitute for legal advice. The area is moving: the MoHFW guidelines were still in draft after public consultation, and the Supreme Court returned to the subject in a judgment of 11 March 2026 permitting withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment in a long-standing persistent vegetative state and urging a legislative framework and more uniform national application. Confirm the current position, and your State’s implementation status, before the pilot goes live.


SOURCE | WHAT IT ESTABLISHES
Common Cause v. Union of India (2018) | A five-judge Constitution Bench held that the right to die with dignity is part of the right to life under Article 21, recognised advance medical directives, and laid down a procedure for withholding and withdrawing life-sustaining treatment in the terminally ill.
Supreme Court order, 24 January 2023 | Simplified that procedure on an application by the Indian Society of Critical Care Medicine. A directive must be in writing and signed by the executor before two attesting witnesses, preferably independent, and attested by a Notary or a Gazetted Officer, who record their satisfaction that it was made voluntarily, free of coercion or inducement, and with full understanding. Counter-signature by a Judicial Magistrate is no longer required, and the District Collector is no longer involved.
Medical boards | For a patient without capacity, a Primary Medical Board is constituted by the hospital for each case — the treating physician and at least two subject experts with five or more years’ experience — and must explain the illness, the treatments available, the alternatives, and the consequences of continuing and of stopping, so that the surrogate is fully informed. A Secondary Medical Board — a practitioner nominated by the District Chief Medical Officer and at least two experts of similar standing, none of whom sat on the Primary Board — then reviews, and is directed to opine within 48 hours.
MoHFW / DGHS draft Guidelines for Withdrawal of Life Support in Terminally Ill Patients (2024) | Issued for public consultation to operationalise the 2018 and 2023 orders. Withdrawal is framed as a considered decision in the patient’s best interests to stop life support that is no longer likely to benefit, or is likely to harm through suffering and loss of dignity. Conditions include brainstem death declared under the THOA, or a prognostic judgement that the condition is advanced and unlikely to benefit from aggressive intervention, together with documented informed refusal following prognostic awareness, and compliance with the Supreme Court’s procedure. Terminal illness is defined as an irreversible or incurable condition from which death is inevitable in the foreseeable future.
ISCCM–IAPC Position Statements (Indian J Crit Care Med 2024;28(3):200–250) | The clinical companion to the law: the end-of-life care pathway, physician reflective prognostic assessment of potentially inappropriate life-sustaining treatment, goals of care set by combining the medical recommendation with the patient’s choices — expressed directly, through a valid advance directive, or through the surrogate — and the safeguards mandated in January 2023 built into the process.
What remains unlawful | Active euthanasia — an intentional act to end life — is illegal in India. Nothing in this record contemplates it. Withholding and withdrawing non-beneficial life support, and giving opioids and sedatives in doses needed to relieve suffering, are lawful and are not euthanasia.


C.1  Where this form sits
–  If a valid attested advance directive exists, it governs. This record operationalises it into specific emergency instructions.
–  If it does not, this record is strong contemporaneous documentation of the patient’s or surrogate’s informed refusal — which is one of the conditions the draft guidelines require — but it does not itself discharge the medical board procedure for withdrawing treatment already in progress.
–  Withholding treatment that has not yet been started sits on a different and simpler footing than withdrawing treatment already running, and most of this form is about withholding. Say this to families: agreeing now not to start the ventilator is a smaller step than stopping one later, and it is the step that protects the patient from the harder decision.

APPENDIX D  —  Running the beta
D.1  Before the first form is used
Ethics approval | Institutional Ethics Committee approval for the pilot, including the consent process, data collection and the version-control plan. Treat it as a service-evaluation protocol with a defined endpoint.
Legal review | Have the form read by the hospital’s legal adviser against the current Supreme Court position and any State-level implementation orders.
Translation | Telugu, Hindi and Urdu versions for the local population, back-translated and checked by a clinician who works in that language. The ED Quick View page must exist in every language in use.
Readability | Target a reading level a Class 8 student can follow. Test the translated version with five families who are not in the study before you use it.
Low literacy | A thumb-impression pathway with two witnesses, and a version read aloud from a script so that every family hears the same words.
Training | No clinician completes this form without observed practice. Budget a half-day: the conversation, not the paperwork, is the intervention.
Retrieval | Decide now how the form is found at 2 a.m.: a flag on the electronic record, a QR code on the wallet card linking to the hospital record, a coloured folder at the bedside. A pilot with no retrieval plan measures nothing.


D.2  Who completes it, and when
–  At the outpatient visit where disease-modifying options run out — the calmest moment, and the one most often missed.
–  At discharge after any unplanned admission in a patient with advanced disease.
–  At ICU step-down, when a patient has survived one episode and the next is foreseeable.
–  On enrolment in home-based palliative care.
–  Not for the first time in the resuscitation bay. A form completed under those conditions records panic, not preference.

D.3  What to measure
DOMAIN | INDICATOR
Reach | Proportion of eligible patients with a completed, signed form; time from eligibility to completion.
Retrieval | Proportion of emergency presentations where the form was located within 10 minutes of arrival. This is the indicator most likely to fail, and the one that determines whether the rest matters.
Concordance | Proportion of episodes where the care delivered matched the recorded ceiling; every discordant episode reviewed individually, with the reason recorded.
Process | Proportion of forms with Section 5 (prognostic understanding) and Section 6 (values) completed rather than left blank while treatment boxes are ticked — the failure mode reported for ReSPECT plans, where clinical recommendations are recorded but the personal-values section is not.
Time-limited trials | Proportion with a documented review at the agreed date; proportion where agreed non-improvement markers were present and the recorded wish was followed.
Outcomes | Place of death against stated preference; ICU days and ventilator days in the last 30 days of life; time from admission to first symptom-control prescription; out-of-pocket expenditure in the last admission.
Experience | Bereaved-family interview at 6–8 weeks: did they feel heard, informed, and unpressured. Clinician survey on confidence and moral distress.
Safety | Every case where the form was applied to a reversible illness, or where a family later said they had felt pushed, reviewed as an incident. Set this up before you start.


D.4  Known risks in this design
–  Ceiling creep: the form makes it easy to record a limit and hard to record why. Section 5 is the guard; audit it.
–  Financial coercion. In a self-pay population, a decision to limit treatment may be about money, not values. Ask directly, record it, and offer the social-work and scheme-eligibility conversation before the treatment conversation is finalised.
–  Surrogate substitution. Families answer with their own preference unless the substituted-judgement declaration in Section 4.3 is read aloud. Read it aloud.
–  The form becoming shorthand for a DNR. Section 16 exists to prevent this; check that ward staff can say what continues, not only what stops.
–  Application to reversible illness. The heading on page 1 is the safeguard, and it must survive any redesign.
–  A form that outlives its accuracy. Preferences recorded three months ago in an outpatient clinic may be wrong today. Enforce the review triggers.
